## I. The Dunning-Kruger Effect and the Fool of the Bible
#### Fools Cannot Recognize the Limits of Their Own Reasoning

<p style="text-indent:2em;">
The Bible warns that fools &ldquo;find no pleasure in understanding&rdquo; the beauty of the natural world, with its ecological interdependence, ecosystem dynamics, and biogeochemical cycles; the transfinite hierarchies of set theory and the recursive, self-similar intricacies of fractal geometry; the elegance of physics, expressed in the symmetries and conservation laws governing matter, energy, and motion, from celestial mechanics to quantum phenomena; the intricate patterns of biology, from the molecular machinery of cells and the regulation of gene expression to the evolutionary mechanisms that shape living systems; the mysteries of chemistry, from electron configurations and molecular bonding to reaction kinetics and the thermodynamic principles governing chemical transformations; and the power and precision of engineering to translate abstract ideas into functioning technologies through mathematical modeling, materials science, control systems, and design. Instead, they &ldquo;delight in airing their own opinions&rdquo; (New International Version Bible, 2011, Prov. 18:2).</p>

<p style="text-indent:2em;">
A related proverb warns that &ldquo;The way of fools seems right to them, but the wise listen to advice&rdquo; (New International Version Bible, 2011, Prov. 12:15). The unwise cannot desire to fathom the formal intricacies of poetry, from prosody, meter, and imagery to the compression of meaning; the grandeur of classical and world literature, from the architecture of epic and the moral complexity of tragedy to the breadth of literary traditions across cultures; the depth of the philosophical tradition, with its enduring debates over being, knowledge, ethics, and political order; the enduring splendor of classical art, with its attention to proportion, balance, and idealized form; or the layered civilizational inheritance of the West, shaped by Greek philosophy, Roman law, Christian thought, and centuries of scientific, artistic, and institutional development. Yet, in a classic Dunning–Kruger pattern, the fool mistakes his own errors for wisdom:</p>

## II. The Need for State College and University Educated Christians
#### Mathematics is the Measure of a Man's Critical Thinking Skills

<p style="text-indent:2em;">
Mathematics is not the sole measure of critical thinking, but it is one of its most rigorous tests. Facione's (1990) expert-consensus report, prepared for the American Philosophical Association's Committee on Pre-College Philosophy, identifies six core critical-thinking skills: interpretation, analysis, evaluation, inference, explanation, and self-regulation (pp. 8–9). Mathematical reasoning exercises several of these skills by requiring a man to follow premises to their conclusions, distinguish valid inference from faulty reasoning, test assumptions, interpret evidence, and justify his conclusions. These habits of mind extend beyond mathematics into the sciences, philosophy, economics, and the evaluation of everyday claims. Mathematics therefore serves as a rigorous means of developing and assessing particular dimensions of critical thinking, even though intellectual maturity requires competencies that extend beyond quantitative reasoning.</p>

If Christian culture cedes this ground, it will not merely lose status&mdash;it will forfeit the chance to shape an AI-assisted golden age of healthcare and engineering.

Just as importantly, it guards him against the Dunning–Kruger effect&mdash;the tendency to overestimate his competence precisely where his knowledge is limited. The foolish man's legacy withers and dies with the limits of his own understanding and opinions because he despised instruction and rejected the wisdom of educated men.</p>


## III. The Consequences of Rejecting Knowledge
#### Envy, Resentment, and the Suppression of Intellectual Growth

<p style="text-indent:2em;">
The pursuit of knowledge requires a willingness to learn from others, including those whose understanding exceeds our own. Yet in some communities, intellectual ambition is met not with encouragement but with ridicule, suspicion, or resentment. When pride makes another person's progress feel like a personal insult, crab mentality and tall-poppy syndrome can become means of discouraging excellence. The problem is not ignorance itself, nor criticism of educated people as such, but the refusal to recognize another's achievement without treating it as a threat to one's own standing.</p>

<p style="text-indent:2em;">
Seneca's observation is not that every critic is envious, but that dissatisfaction with one's own condition can become resentment toward those who advance. His warning remains relevant wherever people discourage learning, belittle intellectual ambition, or seek to diminish those whose achievements expose possibilities they have neglected to pursue.</p>

<p style="text-indent:2em;">
From a Christian perspective, envy also raises a question of spiritual formation. If a person's sense of worth depends on comparing himself with others, another person's success can become a perceived threat rather than an occasion for gratitude. Christian maturity calls believers to resist this impulse: to rejoice in the good of others, pursue wisdom humbly, and seek their own growth without begrudging someone else's.</p>

<p style="text-indent:2em;">
Materialism presents a related but distinct concern. Dittmar et al. (2014), in a meta-analysis of 259 independent samples, found that stronger materialistic values were associated with lower personal well-being. This does not establish that materialism necessarily produces envy, but it supports a broader caution against making possessions and external measures of success the foundation of a meaningful life. Scripture similarly warns that wealth cannot provide ultimate security (Prov. 11:4, ESV).</p>

#### Isolation Instead of Intimacy, Stagnation Instead of Generativity, Despair Instead of Integrity

<p style="text-indent:2em;">
Woe to the man who scoffs at education and mistakes contempt for wisdom. He may believe he is rejecting nothing more than books, universities, or the counsel of educated men, yet the habits he cultivates can reach far beyond the classroom. A man who refuses correction may become too proud to compromise, too dismissive to listen, and too resentful to celebrate another's growth. He risks alienating the very people whose companionship might have enriched his life, sabotaging relationships that require mutual respect and continued growth. He may find himself isolated where he hoped for intimacy, without a lasting partner where he hoped for a family, and with fewer opportunities to contribute meaningfully to the lives of others. His tragedy is not that he lacked credentials, but that pride may have prevented him from becoming the man he had the opportunity to become.
</p>

<p style="text-indent:2em;">
Erikson's theory of psychosocial development gives this warning a sobering structure. As summarized by Nevid (2018), early adulthood presents the challenge of intimacy versus isolation, middle adulthood that of generativity versus stagnation, and late adulthood that of integrity versus despair. Consider what is at stake when a man repeatedly chooses pride over correction and complacency over growth. He may fail to cultivate the intimacy necessary for a lasting partnership; he may reach middle age without having nurtured children, mentored younger people, or built anything intended to benefit the next generation; and he may enter old age with painful awareness of opportunities he dismissed before understanding their value. No single educational choice determines these outcomes, and neither marriage nor parenthood is the sole measure of a meaningful life. Yet a persistent refusal to learn can compound across the years, narrowing the opportunities through which a person might love, serve, create, and leave something of value behind.
</p>

<p style="text-indent:2em;">
This is the danger of stagnation: not merely knowing less than another man, but becoming unwilling to grow when growth remains possible. The man who despises instruction may eventually discover that the world continued to advance while he stood still, that others developed abilities he refused to cultivate, and that opportunities do not remain open indefinitely. He may have no spouse or children, no students he guided, no institution he strengthened, no body of work he brought to maturity, and no legacy he deliberately prepared for those who follow. These outcomes are not inevitable, but they illustrate the cost of allowing pride to govern a life. From a Christian perspective, the warning is not a command to pursue education for status or to measure a man's worth by his family or accomplishments. It is an admonition against burying one's gifts, despising wisdom, and neglecting opportunities to serve God and neighbor through the abilities one could have developed.
</p>

<p style="text-indent:2em;">
The Christian response to anti-intellectualism, then, is not to despise those who know less, but to cultivate a community in which learning is honored, excellence is encouraged, correction is welcomed, and another person's growth is not treated as a personal defeat.</p>
