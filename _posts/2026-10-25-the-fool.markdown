## I. The Dunning-Kruger Effect and the Fool of the Bible
#### Fools Cannot Recognize the Limits of Their Own Reasoning

<p style="text-indent:2em;">
The Bible warns that fools &ldquo;find no pleasure in understanding&rdquo; the beauty of the natural world, with its ecological interdependence, ecosystem dynamics, and biogeochemical cycles; the transfinite hierarchies of set theory and the recursive, self-similar intricacies of fractal geometry; the elegance of physics, expressed in the symmetries and conservation laws governing matter, energy, and motion, from celestial mechanics to quantum phenomena; the intricate patterns of biology, from the molecular machinery of cells and the regulation of gene expression to the evolutionary mechanisms that shape living systems; the mysteries of chemistry, from electron configurations and molecular bonding to reaction kinetics and the thermodynamic principles governing chemical transformations; and the power and precision of engineering to translate abstract ideas into functioning technologies through mathematical modeling, materials science, control systems, and design. Instead, they &ldquo;delight in airing their own opinions&rdquo; (Carson, 2018, Prov. 18:2).</p>

<p style="text-indent:2em;">
A related proverb warns that &ldquo;The way of fools seems right to them, but the wise listen to advice&rdquo; (Carson, 2018, Prov. 12:15). The unwise cannot desire to fathom the formal intricacies of poetry, from prosody, meter, and imagery to the compression of meaning; the grandeur of classical and world literature, from the architecture of epic and the moral complexity of tragedy to the breadth of literary traditions across cultures; the depth of the philosophical tradition, with its enduring debates over being, knowledge, ethics, and political order; the enduring splendor of classical art, with its attention to proportion, balance, and idealized form; or the layered civilizational inheritance of the West, shaped by Greek philosophy, Roman law, Christian thought, and centuries of scientific, artistic, and institutional development. Yet, in a classic Dunning–Kruger pattern, the fool mistakes his own errors for wisdom:</p>

<p style="text-indent:2em;">
Education is vital for nigh-infinite reasons beyond vocation. It equips a man with the depth of skill and expertise to build empires, attract capital, and secure provision sufficient to outpace inflation and preserve his legacy. Just as importantly, it guards him against the Dunning–Kruger effect&mdash;the tendency to overestimate his competence precisely where his knowledge is limited. The foolish man thinks primarily in terms of jobs, asset ownership, and self-employment, economic pursuits that artificial intelligence may one day disrupt or render less valuable. The wise man thinks in terms of <span class="redacted">P1DB8dN4tpIWKsTCkxDD9fr5GAqg74zsJ69gwmGl/fcd92qHNvEyVk2LF/yI73tQFPTwN5jcsK/8C/bm7+L60gk3idE+yeDrl5B7SWLmhNTqEntZ1PF9bVoxoI7a9BNzcfw68Co=</span>. He understands that these are not merely modern ambitions but enduring instruments of economic power, depicted even in Revelation as mighty forces amid the judgments of the end times. He does not merely recognize their significance; he acquires the knowledge and competence necessary to operate within them. Ultimately, the difference is not simply in what these men possess, but in what they understand. The foolish man's legacy withers and dies with the limits of his own understanding and opinions because he despised instruction and rejected the wisdom of educated men:</p>

<p style="text-indent:2em;">
A Christian culture that abandons education and intellectual ambition eventually loses not only cultural influence, but also economic security, institutional leadership, and the ability to reproduce its own institutions and traditions; <strong>Christian minds built science, but now we’re handing it to the godless through the tragedy of blue-collar pride, anti-intellectualism, and backward seminaries, which leads to the death of godly scientific inspiration.</strong> In an AI-driven economy, that failure becomes even more dangerous, because low-skill and routine work are increasingly exposed to automation. As Musk himself acknowledges, there will still be constraints on power, electricity, mass, and other fundamental physics (Shimabukuro, 2025, para. 3); these are not peripheral details but engineering problems. If Christian culture cedes this ground, it will not merely lose status&mdash;it will forfeit the chance to shape an AI-dominated golden age of healthcare and engineering.
</p>

<!-- ## II. Why the Fool's Legacy May Die with His Own Opinions
### The Consequences of Rejecting Knowledge

<p style="text-indent:2em;">
Hosea delivers a sobering warning: “My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge; because you have rejected knowledge, I reject you from being a priest to me. And since you have forgotten the law of your God, I also will forget your children” (ESV Study Bible, 2008, Hos. 4:6; cf. Ro. 3:31).

The context makes this warning especially relevant to those entrusted with educating others. As the *ESV Study Bible* explains, the priests had been charged with teaching the people God's law, yet they had failed in that responsibility. Their rejection of knowledge was not merely a private intellectual failing; it had consequences for the wider community and threatened the spiritual inheritance of the next generation (Vasholz, 2008).

The principle extends beyond the immediate historical context: when those entrusted with teaching become complacent, reject correction, or discourage serious inquiry, their failures can be transmitted to those who depend on them for guidance. The consequences of intellectual neglect are therefore not confined to the individual who refuses to learn. They can shape entire communities, perpetuate error, and deprive future generations of the knowledge and intellectual discipline necessary for growth.

This is one of the most damaging consequences of intellectual neglect: a person may refuse the labor of learning, self-examination, and personal development yet resent those whose efforts have produced genuine growth. Rather than cultivate the qualities they admire in others, they may attempt to diminish the very people who exemplify them. The fool's legacy may then die with his own opinions—not because he lacked the opportunity to learn, but because he refused to let learning correct him. What remains is not a body of knowledge, a record of achievement, or a legacy of service, but a pattern of resentment that discourages others from striving toward excellence.</P> -->
